Noise in the Tahoe Basin: what is measured and what the record shows

TRPA monitors noise in three settings: plan areas across the Basin, the shoreline, and the highway corridors. Each has its own standard, its own sites, and its own cycle. This page gathers the three records in one place.

Plan area noise

Community noise by land use

Plan area monitoring measures the community noise equivalent level (CNEL), a 24-hour average that weights evening and night noise more heavily, at sites grouped by the land use around them. Each land use category has its own standard, from 45 decibels in wilderness and critical wildlife habitat to 65 in industrial areas, and the standards are set as CNEL so that a loud evening counts for more than a loud noon.

Sites are monitored on a rotating schedule, so the record is summarized by monitoring period rather than by year. The chart shows the average CNEL across the sites in four categories for each period since 1987, against each category's standard. Low density residential sites have run at or just under their 50 decibel standard for most of the record; wilderness and roadless sites, held to 45, were above it in the first period and under it since.

Shoreline

Single-event noise on the water

Shoreline monitoring listens for single noise events, mostly from watercraft, at sites around the lake during the peak boating season from July through Labor Day. The standard is a limit on single events, so the measure is how many events per day exceed it rather than an average level. A season with no exceedances meets the standard.

The chart shows the average number of exceedances per day across all shoreline sites for each monitored year since 2009. The record runs between a tenth and a half of an exceedance per day; 2017 was the loudest year monitored, and the two years that followed the pandemic season were among the quietest.

Highways

Traffic noise beside the state routes

Highway monitoring measures CNEL at fixed distances from the edge of each state route, and compares it with a limit set for that highway, 55 decibels on most routes. Sites sit beside U.S. 50, State Routes 28, 89, 207, and 267, and the record notes the distance from the road edge so that readings taken at 100 and 300 feet are not mixed.

The highway record is kept site by site and does not yet have a summary table like the two above. Until it does, the sites and their readings are on the Noise: Highways program page, and the evaluation against the standards is on the Threshold Dashboard.

How noise is measured

Three measures, one unit

CNEL

Community noise equivalent level: a 24-hour average sound level in decibels that adds a penalty of 5 decibels to evening hours and 10 to night hours, because the same sound disturbs more when it is dark and quiet.

Single events

A single noise event is one passing source, a boat or a vehicle, measured at its loudest. Shoreline standards limit single events; an exceedance is one event above the limit.

Standards

TRPA's noise thresholds set a CNEL for each plan area land use category, a single-event limit for the shoreline, and a CNEL limit for each highway. The Threshold Dashboard reports status and trend against them every four years.
